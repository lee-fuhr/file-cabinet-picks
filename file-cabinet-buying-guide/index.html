How to choose the right file cabinet

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

For 2026, focus on drawer suspension quality, material durability, and locking mechanism rather than brand names. A lateral cabinet with full-extension ball-bearing slides offers the best mix of accessibility and stability for most home and small office setups.

What types of file cabinets are available?

Vertical file cabinets are the classic narrow towers (often about 15 inches deep) that fit in tight spots but require you to pull drawers out fully to access files. Lateral cabinets are wider (typically 30 to 42 inches) and let you store files side-to-side, which is easier on your back and often doubles as a credenza. Mobile pedestal file cabinets roll under a desk and hold one or two drawers, great for small spaces. For most people, a lateral cabinet is the most practical choice in 2026 because it offers more usable space per square foot and can also serve as a surface for a printer or decorative items. Vertical cabinets remain useful for cramped home offices, but read owner reviews carefully about tipping risk, they are tall and narrow.

How many drawers do you really need?

Two-drawer cabinets are the sweet spot for a single user with one to two years of paper files or binders. They sit low enough to double as a side table and are easy to move. Three- and four-drawer cabinets hold more but become heavy and harder to open the top drawer unless they are well-designed with anti-tilt mechanisms. A useful rule of thumb: each full drawer of letter-size hanging files holds roughly 25 to 30 folders. If you are going paperless but still need physical storage for tax records and contracts, a two-drawer is plenty. For a bustling family or shared office, a four-drawer might sound efficient, but the weight can make it a hassle, consider buying two two-drawer models instead.

What materials and construction should you look for?

Steel is the most common material and for good reason: it is fire-resistant, dent-resistant, and long-lasting. Look for cabinets with a gauge of 22 or thicker (lower gauge numbers mean thicker steel). Thinner steel (like 24 or 26 gauge) tends to wobble and can bend under the weight of a full drawer. Avoid particleboard models unless you absolutely need a wood finish; they warp and sag over time. Drawer slides matter as much as the cabinet shell. Full-extension ball-bearing slides let you access the entire file without reaching, and they handle heavy loads much better than nylon or steel-on-steel slides. If you see a cabinet described with “ball bearing suspension,” that is a strong sign of quality. Also check that the cabinet has a leveling glide on each foot, uneven floors are common, and a wobbly cabinet is annoying and unsafe.

What about locking mechanisms and security?

Most file cabinets come with a central locking bar that locks all drawers when the key is turned. For basic privacy, a simple cam lock or push-lock with a single key is fine. But if you store sensitive documents (tax returns, medical records, business contracts), look for a cabinet with a tubular lock or a high-security disc lock, those are harder to pick and the keys are not easily duplicated. Also check whether the lock engages both sides of the drawer. Some budget cabinets only lock one side, which leaves the other side vulnerable to prying open. And if you have children in the home, consider a cabinet with a positive interlock system that prevents more than one drawer from being open at a time, this greatly reduces tipping hazard.

How to measure for your space?

Start by measuring the depth of the floor space: lateral cabinets are usually 20 to 22 inches deep (with handles adding an extra inch), while vertical cabinets are around 15 inches. Leave at least 24 inches of clearance in front so you can fully extend a drawer. For width, lateral cabinets come in standard 30-inch and 36-inch widths; make sure the door or hallway to your office can accommodate it. Don’t forget to measure the height of your desk if you plan to place the cabinet underneath it. A typical two-drawer vertical cabinet is about 28 inches tall, which often slides under a standard desk (29-30 inches high). But check the exact under-desk clearance because different desks have aprons or modesty panels that may block a cabinet.

Which suspension system is best?

Ball-bearing suspension is the gold standard. It allows smooth, quiet drawer operation even under heavy loads, and the slides are rated for thousands of cycles. If you see terms like “full-extension ball-bearing slides,” that means the drawer pulls out completely, giving you access to every folder. This is a feature that makes a real difference in daily use, you will curse a shallow slide every time you have to dig for a file. Standard friction slides (also called “stamped steel slides”) work for light use but tend to stick and wear out if the drawer is packed. They are common on budget cabinets. For any cabinet that will see daily action, invest in ball-bearing. For occasional storage of things you rarely touch, friction slides might be acceptable as a cost-saving measure.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a file cabinet for other storage besides hanging files?

Yes. Most file cabinet drawers work fine for mixed storage: you can set hanging frame rails aside to store binders, office supplies, or even tools. Just be aware that lateral cabinets are shallower than vertical ones, so large binders may not fit sideways.

Are lateral cabinets better than vertical ones?

For most people, yes. Lateral cabinets are less prone to tipping, offer more accessible file arrangement, and often include a top surface for a printer or decor. Vertical cabinets save floor width but require more reaching and have a higher center of gravity.

What is the difference between letter-size and legal-size cabinets?

Letter-size cabinets are designed for standard U.S. file folders (9½ by 11¾ inches), while legal-size cabinets accommodate longer folders (9½ by 15¼ inches). A legal-size cabinet can store letter-size folders side-to-side, but not vice versa. If you ever work with legal documents or older folders, go legal-size.

Do I need a fireproof file cabinet?

Only if you are storing irreplaceable documents like birth certificates, contracts, or tax returns. Fire-rated cabinets are heavy, costly, and usually not rated for hours of protection, most provide 30-60 minutes. For true peace of mind, a separate fireproof safe is a better investment.

How much weight can a typical file cabinet hold?

A two-drawer steel cabinet with ball-bearing slides can safely support about 100 to 150 pounds per drawer (a full drawer of paper files is heavy). Budget models with lighter slides may have lower limits. Check the manufacturer’s stated weight capacity before loading, and never exceed it to avoid drawer damage or tip-over.

Should I buy new or used file cabinets?

New cabinets offer consistent build quality, warranty, and modern locking systems. Used steel cabinets from the 1980s or 1990s are often built like tanks, thicker steel and better slides, but may have outdated locks, sticky slides, or missing keys. If you are handy and can inspect in person, a used commercial-grade cabinet can be a fantastic deal.

In shortA good file cabinet is a purchase you will live with for years, so take your time with the decision. Prioritize solid steel construction, ball-bearing full-extension slides, and a security lock that matches your needs. Measure your space honestly, and buy one drawer size larger than you think you need, you will fill it faster than expected.
